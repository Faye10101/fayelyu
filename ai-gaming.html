Overview

Starting with the friction, not the technology

Multiplayer games are full of communication problems — players talking over each other, language barriers, toxic teammates, and no easy way to coordinate strategy in the middle of a match. Our team's question wasn't "what can AI do?" — it was "what do players actually need?"

We conducted nine semi-structured interviews with undergraduate gamers at the University of Melbourne, then used Braun and Clarke's six-phase thematic analysis to find patterns in what they told us. From there, we built personas and conceptual designs grounded in those patterns.

Process

From interviews to insights to design

01

Background research

We reviewed existing gaming AI tools — Mobalytics, NVIDIA's Project G-Assist — to understand what already existed and where the gaps were. Academic literature on communication and toxicity in online games shaped our interview questions.

02

Semi-structured interviews

Nine participants: undergraduate students aged 18–21, all currently playing team-based games. We asked about their communication habits, frustrations, leadership experiences, and views on AI. Each interview ran ~15 minutes, face-to-face on campus.

03

Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke)

We followed the six-phase process: familiarise, generate codes, group into themes, review, name, write up. This gave us a structured way to move from individual answers to bigger patterns about what players really cared about.

04

Personas & scenarios

Each persona was grounded in one of our three identified player types: Joey Zhang (competitive, communication-focused), Alex Phillips (casual, social), and Ahmed (facing language and inclusion barriers). Scenarios made the AI features concrete.

05

Conceptual designs

Three AI features proposed: a Game Plan Summary (condenses teammate chat into a pinned strategy brief), Real-Time Translation (for cross-language teams), and Quick Search (in-game knowledge lookup). Each was tied back to a specific theme and persona.

Key Findings

Four themes that shaped everything

Theme 1

Communication Speed & Clarity

Players need to share key information fast — positions, cooldowns, intentions. Slow or unclear communication directly causes lost rounds.

Theme 2

Frustration with Current Methods

Toxicity, language barriers, and teammates who ignore the group chat all erode the experience. Frustration was consistent across skill levels.

Theme 3

Leadership & Responsibility

Some players avoid leadership roles because of the pressure and blame when things go wrong. Others step up only when teammates are clearly struggling.

Theme 4

Views Towards AI Assistance

Participants were open to AI helping — but only for specific, concrete tasks. Vague AI features didn't resonate; players needed to imagine what it would actually do.

Reflection

What I'd do differently — and what I learned

The research uncovered rich insights about player frustrations, but our questions about AI were too abstract. Participants struggled to imagine what AI could actually do, so their answers were vague. In hindsight, I would have shown a rough sketch of an AI feature before asking about it — giving people something concrete to react to.

This was also my first time conducting face-to-face interviews. I was nervous at the start, but having carefully prepared questions and a team that had rehearsed together made the process feel more manageable. By the third interview I'd found a rhythm.

"The questions about toxicity and frustration got the richest answers — because players had lived those moments. The questions about AI got the most hesitation — because they hadn't."

The strongest thing about our final design concept was recognising that one solution doesn't fit all players. A competitive player wants condensed, fast signals. A casual player might want a coach. That distinction — coming directly from the interviews — felt like a real contribution.
